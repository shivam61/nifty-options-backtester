# Selling Options Works, Until It Doesn't

The global evidence base for systematic options selling is real but narrower than the industry's marketing suggests: the volatility risk premium is one of the most replicated findings in derivatives finance — implied volatility has historically priced in more variance than materializes, so disciplined index-option sellers are on average compensated for underwriting crash insurance ([Bakshi & Kapadia 2003](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v16y2003i2p527-566.html); [Carr & Wu 2009](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf)) — but every well-documented large failure (LJM, Allianz Structured Alpha, Cordier's OptionSellers.com) came from the same negatively-skewed payoff shape the premium is compensation for, and the one academic study found specifically on Nifty straddles reports **negative** Sharpe ratios for both long and short positions ([University of Vaasa thesis](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/handle/11111/19466)). For this backtester, the practical implication is that the system's existing architecture — defined-risk structures, VIX-regime routing, and a hard drawdown kill-switch — already encodes the field's most defensible lessons, while its own chain-engine finding (real NSE settlement prices imply **-177 to -348 Rs/lot** expectancy, "NOT SUPPORTED") should be read as corroborating rather than contradicting the external literature: multiple independent sources (the Vaasa thesis, AlgoTest's warning that simulated backtests understate real costs, and the general Black-Scholes-proxy-vs-real-settle gap) converge on skepticism toward Nifty-specific seller edge once realistic pricing and costs are applied. The sections below work through the track records, the blow-ups, the theory, and the India-specific market structure before landing on eight concrete, evidence-labeled patterns worth prototyping.

## tastytrade's own data contradicts its marketing, and CBOE's benchmark indices win on drawdown, not cleanly on Sharpe

tastylive's (formerly tastytrade's) options-selling methodology — trade near 45 days to expiration, require IV Rank above 50, manage winners at 50% of max profit, size undefined-risk trades smaller than defined-risk ones — is the most widely cited "systematic" retail framework in the world, but it is self-published, non-peer-reviewed brokerage research, and its own studies frequently undercut its own rules. A 2016 SPY iron-condor study found an unmanaged win rate of 53%, rising to 62% when winners were closed at 50% of max profit, and to 70% when an IV Rank > 35 filter was added — but average P/L *dipped slightly* under the 50%-management rule even as win rate rose ([tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/shows/options-jive/episodes/iv-rank-strategy-selection-01-06-2017)). A 2018 follow-up found that trading SPY strangles *only* in high-IV regimes actually **underperformed** trading unconditionally, because VIX sat below 20 roughly 69% of the time and the filter sacrificed too many trades ([tastylive, "Only Trade in High IV"](https://tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/only-trade-in-high-iv-10-22-2018)); a 20-year SPY strangle study split by IV-rank bucket found *low*-IVR trades had the highest win rates but the lowest average profits ([tastylive, Expectancy Trading Strangles](https://www.tastylive.com/news-insights/expectancy-trading-strangles)). The honest reading is that IV-rank filtering and 50%-profit management are variance-reduction and capital-recycling heuristics — they smooth the equity curve and free up margin faster — not reliably expectancy-maximizing rules, a point the firm's own research inadvertently demonstrates. Position-sizing guidance is more directly useful: tastylive recommends defined-risk trades at roughly 1–3% of net liquidating value and undefined-risk (naked) trades at 3–7%, with buying-power requirement treated as a rough proxy for a ~5%-likelihood loss scenario ([tastylive, Defined Risk and Undefined Risk Position Sizing](https://www.tastylive.com/news-insights/Defined-Risk-and-Undefined-Risk-Position-Sizing)); a 2025 Monte Carlo study on iron condors at 1%/5%/10% sizing found larger bets produced better *mean* outcomes but sharply higher risk of ruin — "the riskier the trade, the more important it is to stay small" ([tastylive, Unlocking Kelly with Monte Carlo](https://www.tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/unlocking-kelly-with-monte-carlos-05-06-2025)).

CBOE's own benchmark indices give a longer, less commercially-conflicted track record, though the data is CBOE-sponsored. A study spanning mid-1986 to end-2015 found BXMD (10.66%/yr) and PUT (10.13%/yr) delivering S&P 500-competitive returns with lower volatility (CNDR's iron-condor index had the lowest standard deviation at 7.23%), and far fewer severe-loss months: the S&P 500 recorded 15 months worse than -6% over the period versus 10 for CNDR and just 2 for BFLY ([Hedgeweek](https://www.hedgeweek.com/new-study-analyses-performance-cboe-sp-500-options-selling-indexes/)). But the Sharpe-ratio comparison is genuinely **contested and sample-dependent** — a real conflict in the research, not a rounding error. An older Callan Associates study (1988–2006) found BXM's Sharpe nearly double the S&P 500's, while a separate secondary summary using post-1989 and post-2007 windows reports BXM's Sharpe (0.55) essentially *tying* the S&P 500 (0.56), and PUT's Sharpe (0.51) actually *underperforming* the S&P 500 (0.61) since 2007, with the S&P 500 also winning on Sortino ratio in that source ([cxoadvisory.com](https://cxoadvisory.com/?p=28335)). CBOE's own commentary flags the reason to be cautious either way: Sharpe assumes normally distributed returns, while these indices carry negative skew, so Sharpe comparisons can *overstate* their risk-adjusted attractiveness regardless of which window is used ([CBOE Insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/key-cboe-benchmark-indexes-using-spx-options-offer-strong-risk-adjusted-returns/)). The honest synthesis: options-selling benchmarks reliably cut volatility and tail-loss frequency relative to buy-and-hold; whether they beat it on a risk-adjusted basis depends heavily on whether a major crash falls inside the measurement window, and anyone citing a single Sharpe number for these indices should be asked which period it covers.

The Wheel (cash-secured puts rolled into covered calls on assignment) has essentially **no independent empirical literature** — every source found is retail broker/marketing content describing mechanics, with no peer-reviewed backtest ([Schwab](https://www.schwab.com.sg/story/three-things-to-know-about-wheel-strategy); [research gap confirmed across multiple searches]). The best available evidence is indirect: because the Wheel sequences PUT-style put-writing with BXM-style covered-call writing, the CBOE benchmark data above is a reasonable *proxy*, not a direct finding. 0DTE (zero-days-to-expiration) options have grown explosively — from roughly 5% of SPX options volume in 2016 to a record 66.2% in July 2026 ([FOW/Cboe data](https://www.fow.com/insights/analysis-cboe-points-to-retail-flow-as-zero-day-options-grow)), with retail participation as high as 54% of that volume ([itiger.com](https://www.itiger.com/hans/news/2540584791)) — but the risk/edge question is where sources sharply diverge: Cboe itself (which profits from 0DTE volume, a conflict worth naming) says its data shows no link between 0DTE growth and market turbulence, a Quantpedia summary of an academic study finds the average effect is volatility-*dampening*, yet an unverified secondary account of a Frankfurt School study covering every SPX 0DTE trade from September 2016 to January 2024 claims the captured variance premium is "microscopic" and consumed entirely by transaction costs ([breakingnasdaq.beehiiv.com](https://breakingnasdaq.beehiiv.com/p/the-0dte-illusion)) — a claim this research could not independently verify and that should not be treated as confirmed. Among named funds explicitly harvesting the VRP, the pattern is stark: the two clearest, best-documented failures (LJM, Allianz) both ran *concentrated*, aggressively-scaled short-gamma/short-vega books, while firms still operating without a documented blow-up in these sources — Capstone Investment Advisors (~$8.7–13B AUM), Parallax Volatility Advisers (~$3B), and Neuberger Berman's retail-accessible put-write UCITS fund — appear to run more diversified or defined-size relative-value books, though this is an inference from survivorship, not a directly confirmed structural comparison ([Wikipedia, Capstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capstone_Investment_Advisors); [Neuberger Berman fund page](https://nb.com/en/gb/products/ucits-funds/us-equity-premium-fund)).

## Every major blow-up shares one trait: discretion overrode the risk rule exactly when it mattered most

February 2018's "Volmageddon" is the canonical mechanical failure. Leveraged/inverse VIX ETNs (XIV, SVXY) had to buy VIX futures as VIX rose to maintain constant daily leverage — a short-gamma rebalancing requirement that grows mechanically with the size of the move and the size of the fund — and the rebalance was compressed into a narrow window just before the 16:15 NAV mark, letting front-running traders amplify the very spike the funds then had to chase ([CFA Institute FAJ summary](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2021/volmageddon-failure-short-volatility-products)). XIV lost roughly 97% of its value (~$2 billion) in a single day; LJM Partners, a Chicago fund explicitly running a short-volatility strategy, saw assets collapse from $812 million to $14 million in a month, with trading losses exceeding $1 billion ([Bloomberg, "The Day The Vix Doubled"](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2019-02-06/the-day-the-vix-doubled-tales-of-volmageddon)). Critically, LJM did not control its own exit: a federal judge found its prime-broker contract with Wells Fargo gave the bank "broad authority" to direct trading in a stressed market, and blocked LJM's suit against the bank ([BusinessInsurance.com](https://www.businessinsurance.com/judge-blocks-chicago-fund-ljm-partners-lawsuit-against-wells-fargo/)). The lesson for any backtester is specific: a model that assumes the trader controls exit execution and timing in a crisis will understate real losses relative to what a forced, broker-directed liquidation produces — and the fact that the ETN rebalance was concentrated right before market close is a reminder that "mid-session is calmer" assumptions (directly relevant to this system's 11:00–13:00 IST entry window) say nothing about what happens later in the same session.

Allianz Global Investors' "Structured Alpha" funds are the most structurally relevant precedent for a system that explicitly uses VIX-regime circuit breakers, because the funds were **designed** with purchased tail hedges on paper. They held over $11 billion in AUM and lost more than $7 billion in February–March 2020; investors alleged the hedges meant to limit losses were quietly stripped out before the crash, with one suit calling the fund's positioning "AllianzGI's reckless throw of the dice" ([Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/b1mt7hvffkyw84/Allianz-s-Volatility-Fund-Problems-Get-Bigger); [Law360](https://www.law360.com/classaction/articles/1306887)). In May 2022 Allianz Global Investors U.S. pled guilty to criminal securities fraud, was barred from advising U.S.-registered funds for a decade, and the group paid roughly $6 billion in combined fines, restitution, and forfeiture ([Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/b1y2lj4jkfmvzc/Charged-With-Fraud-by-SEC-and-DOJ-Allianz-Settles-by-Paying-Billions)). The failure mode was not that short-vol-with-hedges is unhedgeable — it is that the hedge ratio was a governance/discretion variable, removable under P&L pressure, rather than a mechanically enforced rule. The Karen "the Supertrader" Bruton case adds a different, equally important lesson about survivorship bias in published track records rather than about the strategy itself: Bruton, publicized by Tastytrade for selling large-size index strangles, was charged by the **SEC** (not CFTC) in 2016 for allegedly concealing over $50 million of trading losses rather than recognizing them; she settled in 2018 for disgorgement of $1.24 million and a $250,000 penalty plus an industry bar ([SEC Litigation Release No. 24285](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/litreleases/2018/lr24285.htm)). A widely circulated "proof that naked strangle-selling works" turned out to rest on hidden losses, not a verified edge — a direct warning against taking any unaudited educator track record (Indian or American) at face value. James Cordier's OptionSellers.com (co-author of *The Complete Guide to Option Selling*) rounds out the picture: naked commodity-option selling blew up in November 2018 when natural gas spiked ~20% in a day, costing roughly $150 million across ~290 client accounts that were forcibly liquidated by the clearing broker ([optionstradingiq.com](https://optionstradingiq.com/how-to-lose-100-million/); [Wolf Street](https://wolfstreet.com/2018/12/17/us-natural-gas-plunges-unwinds-spike-that-blew-up-hedge-fund/)) — notably, Cordier himself had publicly warned about natural-gas tail risk months before his own fund's collapse, proving that *awareness* of tail risk is not the same as *sizing against it*.

Across these cases and the practitioner sources that discuss surviving them, five risk controls recur: size undefined-risk positions materially smaller than defined-risk ones (the 1–3%/3–7% NAV bands above); scale size down as VIX/India VIX rises past threshold bands (halving around VIX 25, cutting to a third above 35 per various discretionary practitioner rules — none of these specific thresholds were independently backtested in the sources found, which is itself worth flagging); maintain tail hedges as a standing, rules-triggered cost rather than a discretionary add-on (one estimate puts a rolling far-OTM-put hedge at ~3.6% of notional per year); treat simultaneous drawdowns or rising cross-asset correlation as a systemic-risk signal distinct from any single position's loss; and enforce hard limits (max drawdown, position caps, kill switches) in deterministic code outside any model or discretionary override ([practitioner source via search summary](https://playbooks.com/skills/omer-metin/skills-for-antigravity/risk-management-trading)). None of these numeric thresholds are independently backtested in the sources themselves — they are stated as heuristics by options educators or risk practitioners with varying degrees of commercial interest — which means a systematic backtester is better positioned to *test* them empirically than to adopt them on authority. Notably, no documented, named, large-scale Indian proprietary short-vol fund blow-up (an "LJM of India") surfaced in open search despite targeted queries; Indian financial media instead emphasizes aggregate retail options-*buyer* losses and expiry-day concentration risk (59% of FY26 index-options turnover occurred on expiry day), and the one crore-scale Nifty-options loss event found was a June 2022 fat-finger mis-trade (Vardhaman Global, ~₹200–250 crore), not a short-vol strategy failure ([Moneylife](https://www.moneylife.in/article/fat-finger-trade-in-nifty-options-on-thursday-leads-to-a-loss-of-rs200-rs250-crore/67385.html)) — this absence should be read as a genuine evidentiary gap, not evidence of safety, given that only ~2% of individual Indian F&O traders are primarily sellers and detailed loss attribution by trader type is not publicly reported.

## The volatility risk premium is real and replicated, but its size is not constant and it is not universal

The academic case for a positive expectancy from selling index options rests on three independently converging empirical approaches, which is unusually strong corroboration by the standards of empirical finance. Bakshi and Kapadia's delta-hedged option-return methodology found significantly negative average returns to *holding* S&P 500 options (i.e., sellers who delta-hedge are compensated), with the effect largest for at-the-money options and in high-volatility periods ([Bakshi & Kapadia 2003, *Review of Financial Studies*](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v16y2003i2p527-566.html)). Carr and Wu's model-free, variance-swap-based construction found a historically negative variance risk premium across five stock indexes (implied variance exceeding subsequently realized variance) ([Carr & Wu 2009](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf)). Bollerslev, Tauchen, and Zhou showed this same premium *predicts* aggregate equity returns at roughly a quarterly horizon, outperforming standard predictors like the P/E ratio ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=948309)), and a 2014 *JFQA* follow-up confirmed the result is not a small-sample artifact and extends internationally via a "global VRP" factor ([Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/732839)). A concrete magnitude: using daily S&P 500 data from 1990–2007, one-month at-the-money options implied roughly 19% annualized volatility on average against ~15.7% subsequently realized — implied vol priced roughly 18% "too high" on average ([Eraker, via cxoadvisory.com](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/the-why-of-the-volatility-risk-premium)), and a separate 1996–2014 CFA-digested study found the premium positive in **every** VIX decile, with a delta-hedged long 5%-OTM put strategy losing money in every bucket studied — i.e., buying downside protection was a net cost in every regime examined ([CFA Institute digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2015/12/still-not-cheap-portfolio-protection-in-calm-markets-digest-summary)). But the premium is explicitly **not universal**: a KOSPI200 study applying the same Bakshi-Kapadia methodology to Korean index options did *not* find a significant at-the-money volatility risk premium, though OTM puts did show negative delta-hedged gains attributed partly to overpricing/microstructure effects ([Emerald/JDQS](https://www.emerald.com/jdqs/article/17/2/67/205260/The-Volatility-Risk-Premium-in-the-KOSPI200-Index)) — direct evidence that the premium's existence, not just its size, is market-structure-dependent, which matters for extrapolating U.S./S&P 500 findings to Nifty.

Why does the premium exist? The literature layers three explanations rather than settling on one: classic insurance demand (risk-averse pension funds, insurers, and portfolio managers paying for crash protection, consistent with Bollerslev and Todorov's finding that rare-disaster risk explains a large share of the equity premium itself — [Acadian](https://www.acadian-asset.com/investment-insights/managing-risk/crash-risk-hedgers-vs-harvesters)); a mechanical, structural hedging-flow channel (dynamic/delta hedging of insurer guarantee books requires selling into falling markets, itself amplifying realized volatility and creating demand for protection — [Wharton/Jacobs Levy](https://jacobslevycenter.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/2019.12-Insurance-against-Long-Run-Volatility-Risk.pdf)); and dealer/intermediary funding constraints, where Barras and Malkhozov found the gap between option-implied and equity-market-based variance risk premia tracks derivatives-dealer balance-sheet health — meaning part of the "edge" is compensation for warehousing risk when dealer capacity is scarce, not pure end-investor risk aversion ([systemicrisk.ac.uk](https://www.systemicrisk.ac.uk/sites/default/files/media/Malkhozov_0.pdf)). This last point matters for robustness: if intermediary capacity changes (post-crisis deleveraging, a vol-ETP unwind), the measured premium should be expected to shift for reasons unrelated to insurance demand. The premium also compresses or reverses outright during realized-vol spikes — CBOE's own research flags 2008 as the chief historical exception year for SPX put-writing's realized premium ([CBOE Insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/key-cboe-benchmark-indexes-using-spx-options-offer-strong-risk-adjusted-returns/)) — and separately from the average-premium question, the payoff is fundamentally asymmetric: a Pepperdine working paper on SPY short strangles found that a single bad position's loss can equal the cumulative profit of roughly **forty** prior winning positions ([Pepperdine Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.pepperdine.edu/graziadiowps/25)). This is the rigorous underpinning for the "picking up pennies in front of a steamroller" metaphor used by both critics and some proponents, and it is formally why naive full-Kelly position sizing is dangerous for this payoff shape: fat tails and negative skew mechanically shrink the Kelly-optimal fraction relative to a thin-tailed asset ([arXiv, "Leverage and Uncertainty"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1612.07194)). A 2025 study directly testing Kelly, VIX-regime, and hybrid sizing on short-dated index put-writing found the hybrid approach "consistently balances return generation with robust drawdown control" better than either pure method alone ([arXiv 2508.16598](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.16598v1)) — the single most directly applicable academic source found for this project's entry-threshold/VIX-gate design, though it is one paper, not independently replicated, and its full text could not be retrieved beyond the abstract-level summary in this research. Nassim Taleb's long-standing critique that "option sellers eat like chickens and go to the bathroom like elephants" is genuinely contested at the academic level, not a settled consensus against short-vol: AQR's Antti Ilmanen published a direct rebuttal arguing Taleb's confidence in his priors was "excessive" and unsupported by sufficient data, a dispute that resurfaced publicly in a 2020 exchange between Taleb and Cliff Asness ([AQR author response](https://www.aqr.com/insights/research/journal-article/do-financial-markets-reward-buying-or-selling-insurance-and-lottery-tickets-author-response)) — the tail risk itself is not in dispute (it is empirically realized repeatedly), but how it should be *priced* relative to the premium remains an open, credentialed disagreement.

The book landscape splits cleanly by rigor and by what happened to the authors. Natenberg's *Option Volatility and Pricing* is confirmed by multiple secondary sources to teach that a seller's edge comes from selling when implied volatility exceeds one's best estimate of future realized volatility, with explicit vega/theta risk framing rather than naive probability-of-profit reasoning — but this research could not retrieve primary text beyond generic, multiply-mirrored publisher summaries, which is a real sourcing limitation worth naming rather than papering over. Cordier and Gross's *The Complete Guide to Option Selling* is a popular, non-technical advocate for selling (often naked) OTM options, with chapter titles including "SPAN margin: the key to high returns" and "Risk control in option selling" — but co-author James Cordier's own fund collapsed in exactly the manner the academic skew-premium literature predicts, a direct, verifiable real-world contradiction between the book's implicit promise and its author's practice that this research treats as the single most important caveat on that book. Euan Sinclair's *Volatility Trading* occupies the useful middle ground: Sinclair insists a trader must be able to articulate *why* implied volatility is priced where it is before trading it, and states that behavioral biases are "probably the drivers behind most sources of edge available to a volatility trader" — explicitly endorsing a behavioral-overpayment explanation alongside the pure risk-premium one, and implicitly warning against mechanically harvesting a premium whose source one cannot name.

## SEBI's 2024-2025 reforms, no VIX options, and a sell-side STT make India structurally different from the U.S.

Nifty and Bank Nifty options share SPX's core mechanical advantage — cash settlement with no early-assignment risk — but diverge sharply on cost and hedging infrastructure. SEBI's late-2024 reform package, effective 20 November 2024, cut weekly expiries to one benchmark index per exchange (NSE kept Nifty only, discontinuing Bank Nifty, Nifty Financial Services, and Nifty Midcap Select weeklies), roughly tripled the minimum contract value (from ~₹5 lakh to ₹15–20 lakh), added a 2% Extreme Loss Margin on short index-options positions on expiry day, and introduced intraday position-limit monitoring ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/markets/news/sebi-announces-six-key-changes-to-curb-speculation-in-derivatives-trading-124100101316_1.html)). Bank Nifty weeklies had represented almost half of NSE's weekly-options premium turnover before discontinuation, and NSE's own CEO publicly predicted that volume would not simply migrate to Bank Nifty monthlies but would partly "disappear" ([5paisa](https://5paisa.com/news/sebi-directive-to-end-bank-nifty-weekly-options-impact-on-nse-volumes)) — a prediction broadly confirmed by Nifty subsequently overtaking Bank Nifty as the world's most-traded equity index options contract (though this specific ranking claim comes from a lower-confidence secondary source and was not independently verified against a primary FIA/WFE report). Mandatory upfront premium payment followed on 1 February 2025, calendar-spread margin benefits were removed on 10 February 2025, and intraday position-limit enforcement rolled out in two phases through 2025, culminating in an entity-level net intraday limit of ₹5,000 crore (up from ₹1,500 crore end-of-day) with an expiry-day penalty regime effective 6 December 2025 ([SCC Online](https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2025/09/03/sebi-equity-derivatives-intraday-limits-2025/)). Lot sizes were revised too — Nifty to 65 units (matching this backtester's own `lot_size` constant) and Bank Nifty to 30 units, effective with the January 2026 contract ([Algotest](https://algotest.in/blog/nifty-lot-size.md)) — and the weekly/monthly expiry weekday itself moved from Thursday to Tuesday starting 2 September 2025, which this repository's own `_TUESDAY_CUTOVER` constant already reflects. The net effect of the reform package is to raise the capital and operational bar for running a systematic Nifty options-selling book, explicitly favoring well-capitalized, rules-based players over small discretionary retail sellers — which appears to be SEBI's intended effect, not an incidental one, and which structurally validates this backtester's monthly-track-only-with-single-weekly-index design as already aligned with the post-reform tradable instrument set (Bank Nifty no longer has any weekly optionality to backtest against going forward).

Two India-specific structural features have no clean U.S. analog and deserve explicit modeling attention. First, a sell-side-specific Securities Transaction Tax: STT on the sale of options premium rose from 0.0625% to 0.1% effective 1 October 2024, and a further Budget-reported hike to 0.15% was set to take effect 1 April 2026 ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/budget/news/budget-2024-steep-stt-increase-to-tame-retail-frenzy-in-derivatives-market-124072301145_1.html); [Outlook Money](https://www.outlookmoney.com/amp/story/invest/stt-hike-from-april-1-2026-budget-what-it-means-for-futures-and-options-traders), unverified against a primary notification) — a direct, recurring transaction cost levied specifically on the writing leg of every trade, with no U.S. federal equivalent, that disproportionately penalizes high-frequency rolling strategies (precisely the weekly-track pattern this system runs). Second, and more consequentially for risk management, no source confirmed that India VIX **options** exist on NSE at all — only India VIX futures, launched February 2014 — meaning Indian systematic sellers have no direct, exchange-listed way to hedge "vol of vol" the way a U.S. trader can partially hedge with VIX options or calls funding an SPX put spread. Any tail hedge in India must be constructed synthetically from far-OTM Nifty puts, and those are also the instruments most likely to become illiquid or frozen against wide dynamic price bands exactly when a hedge is needed most (fast, large moves) — a structural fragility with no direct parallel in the CBOE/U.S. toolkit, and one this research could not quantify with current bid-ask spread data because no rigorous, current Nifty-vs-SPX spread study was found.

The Indian educator and platform ecosystem offers essentially no audited evidence base to lean on. P R Sundar, the most prominent Indian options-trading YouTube educator, is explicitly not SEBI-registered and was barred from trading for one year in May 2023 over unregistered investment-advisory activity ([Storyboard18](https://stgcms.storyboard18.com/?p=8483)); forum commentary (unverified, anecdotal) describes the common pattern among such educators as active manual "firefighting"/adjustment of losing legs rather than static defined-risk structures — a style that is inherently harder to backtest and more exposed to selective, survivorship-biased self-reporting. Sensibull and Quantsapp are analytics/tooling platforms, not published strategies; Sensibull's own marketing has stated that "less than 1% of options traders make any money in the long run" ([Zerodha Z-Connect](https://zerodha.com/z-connect/tradezerodha/sensibull/introducing-sensibull-the-options-trading-platform)), and even a retail Udemy course selling an "adjustment" methodology concedes that "without adjustment, 75% of the [short] strangle will end up in loss" ([Udemy](https://www.udemy.com/course/nifty-option-selling-monthly-and-weekly/)) — an admission against interest from inside the educator ecosystem itself. This pattern is corroborated at scale by SEBI's own repeated studies: 93% of individual F&O traders lost money in FY22–FY24 (aggregate losses over ₹1.8 lakh crore), over 91% lost money in FY25 (net losses ₹105,603 crore), and roughly 88% of individual options traders lost money in FY26, with options accounting for ~92% of aggregate individual-trader losses and 97% of individual traders being primarily *buyers*, not sellers, of options ([Moneylife](https://www.moneylife.in/article/93-percentage-of-individual-traders-lost-rs18-lakh-crore-in-equity-fo-in-past-3-years-sebi/75210.html); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/markets/news/net-losses-of-traders-in-fo-widens-in-fy25-sebi-study-125070701221_1.html)). The one genuinely academic Indian-market source found, a University of Vaasa thesis testing delta-neutral long and short straddles on Nifty 50, reported **negative Sharpe ratios for both strategies** — no evidence of a risk-adjusted seller edge — while explicitly flagging a limited data sample ([University of Vaasa](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/handle/11111/19466)). That finding sits in direct tension with the globally-replicated VRP literature above, and this research cannot resolve the tension (the thesis was read only via abstract-level summary, not in full) — but it is strikingly consistent with this backtester's own documented chain-engine result using real NSE settlement prices (-177 to -348 Rs/lot across slippage scenarios, pre-registered verdict "NOT SUPPORTED"), and with AlgoTest's general warning that Indian options backtests commonly ignore brokerage, taxes, and slippage in ways that make simulated P&L look better than live P&L would ([AlgoTest](https://algotest.in/blog/how-to-backtest-a-strategy-on-an-option-simulator.md)). Two independent sources — one academic, one this project's own internal validation — now point the same direction, which should raise, not lower, the bar of proof required before trusting any Black-Scholes-proxy-derived positive-CAGR result from `--mode evolve` or `--mode backtest-combined` as evidence of a real, tradable edge.

## Eight patterns worth prototyping, ranked by how well they're actually supported

The table below distills the research into concrete next steps, each labeled by evidence strength (well-evidenced = multiple independent, ideally peer-reviewed or long-run sources agree; mixed evidence = credible sources conflict or results are sample-dependent; anecdotal/survivorship-biased = heuristic, self-reported, or drawn mainly from survivors' own accounts) and tied to a specific, testable change in this codebase.

| Pattern | What it is | Evidence strength & why | How to test/adapt here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mechanical, non-overridable hedge/kill-switch enforcement** | Risk limits (hedge ratios, drawdown caps) enforced in code with no discretionary override | **Well-evidenced by contrast**: Allianz's Structured Alpha failure was specifically a *discretionary* removal of a hedge that existed on paper ([Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/b1mt7hvffkyw84/Allianz-s-Volatility-Fund-Problems-Get-Bigger)); LJM's exit was controlled by its broker's contractual discretion, not its own rules ([BusinessInsurance.com](https://www.businessinsurance.com/judge-blocks-chicago-fund-ljm-partners-lawsuit-against-wells-fargo/)) | The `dd_kill_pct`/`dd_recovery_pct` kill-switch and VIX gates already follow this philosophy (cf. the cache-integrity guardrails' "no env var disables this" design). Extend the same no-override discipline explicitly to any future hedge-overlay logic and to `--mode signal-combined`'s live gates, and add a regression test asserting the kill-switch cannot be bypassed by any CLI flag or config path. |
| **Defined-risk sizing smaller than undefined-risk, with explicit % bands** | Cap undefined-risk (naked) exposure near 3–7% of NAV, defined-risk near 1–3% | **Anecdotal/heuristic but directionally well-corroborated by blow-up pattern**: Karen Bruton, Cordier, and (structurally) LJM all ran concentrated undefined/large-size naked or near-naked books; tastylive's own numbers are self-published, non-backtested recommendations ([tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/news-insights/Defined-Risk-and-Undefined-Risk-Position-Sizing)) | This system already uses defined-risk structures exclusively (IC, PCS, BWB), so the acute naked-strangle risk is structurally avoided. Worth doing: audit `monthly_max_lots_cap`/per-trade margin against the 1–3% NAV band explicitly, and log per-trade margin-as-%-of-NAV in `backtest_runs.jsonl` to make this auditable going forward. |
| **VIX/India-VIX-regime position-size scaling** | Cut size as VIX rises past thresholds (e.g., halve above a threshold, cut to a third above a higher one) | **Mixed evidence**: these are discretionary practitioner heuristics with no independent backtest found, but conceptually validated by the one rigorous paper on this exact problem (below) | Run a parameter sweep in `--mode evolve` testing India-VIX-banded size multipliers (e.g., 100%/75%/50% at VIX breakpoints ~13/17) against the current fixed `monthly_max_lots_cap`, using `--mode ablation` to isolate the sizing rule's marginal contribution. |
| **Hybrid fractional-Kelly + VIX-regime sizing** | Size positions via a fractional-Kelly base, modulated by a VIX-regime multiplier, rather than either alone | **Mixed evidence — single paper, directly on-point**: a 2025 arXiv study on exactly this problem (index put-writing) found the hybrid approach "consistently balances return generation with robust drawdown control" better than pure Kelly or pure VIX sizing ([arXiv 2508.16598](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.16598v1)), but this is one unreplicated paper whose full text was not retrieved | Fetch the full paper and prototype its sizing rule as an alternative `PositionSizer` variant (see `tests/test_position_sizer.py`), A/B'd against the current fixed 50/50 monthly/weekly split and 30-lot cap across the same walk-forward windows used in `--mode validate`. |
| **Standing tail-hedge overlay, synthetic (far-OTM puts)** | A small, continuously-maintained book of far-OTM puts funded from premium, as insurance rather than a discretionary add-on | **Well-evidenced in principle, structurally harder in India**: crash-risk/skew-premium literature supports paying for insurance ([Acadian](https://www.acadian-asset.com/investment-insights/managing-risk/crash-risk-hedgers-vs-harvesters)), but no India VIX options exist and far-OTM Nifty puts are the most illiquid strikes exactly when needed | Test whether a rules-triggered (not discretionary) rolling far-OTM Nifty put position, costed at roughly the ~3.6%/yr benchmark found for the U.S. analog, measurably reduces frequency/severity of `dd_kill_pct` triggers — but validate against real NSE settlement liquidity (chain engine), not Black-Scholes-proxy fills, given the documented illiquidity risk at far-OTM strikes. |
| **Cross-track correlation/simultaneous-drawdown trigger** | Treat simultaneous losses or rising correlation across the monthly and weekly tracks as a distinct de-risking signal, separate from level-based VIX/DD triggers | **Anecdotal, not rigorously validated**: practitioner sources describe correlation-spike de-risking as sound practice, but no backtested study of its efficacy for options-selling books specifically was found | The combined engine already runs monthly+weekly concurrently and has a `vix_simultaneous_cap`; add an explicit same-day-loss-correlation flag between the two tracks, logged separately, and test whether it would have added value beyond the existing VIX cap in past drawdown episodes. |
| **Treat Black-Scholes-proxy backtest P&L as an upper bound, not an expected return** | Discount headline `--mode evolve`/`backtest-combined` CAGR figures until validated against real settlement prices | **Well-evidenced within this project's own data**: the chain engine's real-NSE-settle result (-177 to -348 Rs/lot, "NOT SUPPORTED") and the University of Vaasa thesis's negative Sharpe for Nifty straddles both point the same direction, independently; AlgoTest separately warns simulated backtests ignore real costs | Prioritize extending real-price chain-engine coverage (more strategies, more strikes/expiries) as the gating validation step before trusting any *new* strategy's BS-proxy backtest result; report both BS-proxy and chain-engine figures side-by-side in `BACKTEST_CHANGELOG.md` for every run going forward. |
| **0DTE-style same-day entries** | Opening and closing short-premium positions same-day rather than on a weekly/monthly cycle | **Mixed-to-negative evidence, and a poor India fit**: Cboe's own volume growth story is well corroborated, but the risk/edge conclusion is contested (an unverified Frankfurt School claim reports a "microscopic," cost-consumed premium on SPX 0DTE), and India's sell-side-specific STT hike makes high-frequency same-day rolling structurally more expensive than in the U.S., with no comparable federal tax | Do not prioritize prototyping same-day entries; the existing weekly/monthly cadence is both better evidenced and better aligned with India's cost structure. If tested anyway, model the sell-side STT explicitly per round-trip, not as a flat basis-point assumption, given it scales with premium collected, not just notional. |

## What this changes about how to read the backtester's own numbers

The deepest finding here is not a specific tactic but a calibration point: this is one of the few domains where the "systematic edge" claimed by the retail education industry (tastylive's self-contradicting studies, Indian YouTube educators, Cordier's own book) is simultaneously the subject of a genuinely replicated academic literature (Bakshi-Kapadia, Carr-Wu, Bollerslev-Tauchen-Zhou) *and* a graveyard of well-documented failures in funds that took the premium's existence as license to under-hedge or over-concentrate. The premium is real for U.S. index options across multiple independent methodologies; it is explicitly not confirmed for Nifty by the one academic study found, and it reversed outright for the global benchmark in 2008. That means the burden of proof for this backtester's design choices should sit closer to "prove it survives a chain-engine validation on real settlement prices" than to "prove it beats a Black-Scholes-proxy backtest," because the external literature and this project's own internal evidence now agree on exactly that point from two independent directions. The system's existing architecture — defined-risk legs, VIX-regime circuit breakers, a hard (not discretionary) drawdown kill-switch — already embodies the single clearest lesson from every documented blow-up in this research; the highest-value next step is not a new strategy but tightening the loop between the Black-Scholes-proxy backtest that currently drives `--mode evolve` and the real-price chain engine that currently contradicts it.
