## 1. Santa-Clara & Saretto (2009): what was tested, data, margin model, costs, results

### Takeaway
Selling OTM options (especially near-maturity strangles) shows very high gross Sharpe ratios, but round-trip bid-ask costs plus a realistic margin-call model cut them sharply, to near zero or negative for the best strategies. The abstract frames the practical lesson as: short-option returns are a "good deal" that frictions make hard to capture, and the effect is stronger the more aggressively options are written. I could not verify from any snippet that defined-risk condors or butterflies were tested.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Pedro Santa-Clara and Alessio Saretto, "Option strategies: Good deals and margin calls", Journal of Financial Markets, vol. 12, no. 3 (2009), pp. 391-417; earlier working version dated January 2006. Read: abstract and snippet excerpts only, not full text — [IDEAS/RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v12y2009i3p391-417.html), [SSRN](https://ssrn.com/abstract=681643), [UCLA copy](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/santa_clara_option.pdf)
- Strategies examined (per summaries): naked and covered positions, straddles, strangles, and calendar spreads, with different maturities and levels of moneyness — [search summary of IDEAS/UCLA/eScholarship pages](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/santa_clara_option.pdf). Butterflies and condors: not confirmed in any result I saw (the summary explicitly said it found no butterfly/condor mention). Treat as unverified, not as "absent".
- Data (working-paper version): S&P 500 options, January 1985 to December 2002. The main dataset is from the Institute for Financial Markets: daily closing prices of American options on S&P 500 futures traded at the CME, January 1985 to May 2001. The paper also uses OptionMetrics data for European S&P 500 index options — [search summary of the working paper](http://www.econ.yale.edu/~shiller/behfin/2005-04/santa-clara-saretto.pdf). A later version is described as covering "January of 1985 to April of 2006" — [same search family](https://conference.nber.org/confer/2005/bfs05/saretto.pdf). Sample period therefore differs by version; the published-JFM period is a gap. Frequency: daily closing prices, monthly holding-period returns appear to be used (monthly mean return quoted below); not confirmed by full text.
- Abstract-level headline (working version): "strategies involving short positions in options generally compensate the investor with very high Sharpe ratios," as high as 1.69 annualized for a near-maturity strangle, statistically significant even after allowing for non-normality, and "substantially higher than warranted by asset pricing models" — [search summary of SSRN/UCLA abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=681643)
- Abstract-level headline (published version): trading frictions have an economically important impact on the execution and profitability of strategies that involve writing OTM puts; margin requirements "limit the notional amount of capital that can be invested in the strategies and force investors to close down positions and realize losses"; the effect is stronger when the investor writes more aggressively — [search summary of JFM/econbiz abstract](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/option-strategies-good-deals-and-margin-calls-santa-clara-pedro/10004973479)
- Margin calls: taking margin calls into account turns the Sharpe ratio of some of the best strategies negative; the authors also suggest exchange margin requirements may be set too high relative to the actual risk of the positions — [search summary](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0499w44p)
- Margin model: for a naked position, margin = option proceeds + a percentage of the underlying value, less any OTM amount. CBOE parameters are alpha = 15% and beta = 10%; a retail investor at a web broker is contrasted with alpha = 40% and beta = 35%. The CBOE manual describes the index version as 100% of option proceeds plus 15% of the underlying index value — [search summary of paper](http://www.econ.yale.edu/~shiller/behfin/2005-04/santa-clara-saretto.pdf), [CBOE Margin Manual](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/membership/Margin_Manual.pdf). Which parameter is which (alpha vs beta) and the exact formula are not verified.
- Margin burden: on average an investor must deposit $3.9 of margin (in addition to sale proceeds) per dollar received from shorting far-maturity ATM calls; for near-maturity 10% OTM calls $43.1 of margin per dollar of premium — [search summary of paper](http://www.econ.yale.edu/~shiller/behfin/2005-04/santa-clara-saretto.pdf)
- Transaction costs: bid-ask spreads are modeled; Sharpe ratios "decrease substantially, and sometimes even turn negative". Spreads are wider for OTM and near-maturity options: a 5% OTM call has a round-trip cost equal to 17.4% of price near maturity versus 2.7% far from expiration — [search summary](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/santa_clara_option.pdf)
- Moneyness gradient: Sharpe ratios rise with moneyness; near-maturity 10% OTM strangle 0.654 versus 0.247 for the ATM straddle. Units not stated in the snippet (likely monthly, not annualized — unverified) — [search summary](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/option_strategies.pdf)
- Near-maturity 5% OTM strangle: Sharpe 0.474 and mean return 2.7% per month without margin calls; with margin calls the average return becomes -1.0% per month. A separate sentence in the same summary says the Sharpe "drops from 0.454 once costs are included" but gives no post-cost value, so that sentence is incomplete — [search summary](https://conference.nber.org/confer/2005/bfs05/saretto.pdf)
- Third-party write-up: with transaction costs and margin calls, the annualized Sharpe of shorting near-maturity OTM strangles falls from 1.83 to 0.16 — [CXO Advisory summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/good-deals-in-broad-market-index-options/), [CXO ?p=965](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=965). This 1.83 conflicts with the "1.69" in the abstract summary; they may refer to different versions or different strangle definitions. A search specifically for "1.83 OR 0.16 OR 1.69" in the paper could not confirm 1.83 or 0.16 from paper text (the 1.69 figure was found).
- Stop-loss style risk limits: I found nothing on stop-loss rules in any snippet. The paper's risk limit, as summarized, is the margin-call mechanism itself (forced liquidation), not a discretionary stop.

### Inferences
- The margin/forced-liquidation path is the dominant friction in this paper, not just the bid-ask spread. A backtester that ignores margin-driven liquidation will overstate short-strangle performance in the same way the gross figures do.
- The $3.9 vs $43.1 margin-per-premium-dollar contrast shows margin intensity per dollar of credit rises steeply as you go further OTM and nearer expiry, which is the same corner (near-maturity, far OTM) with the highest gross Sharpe and the highest round-trip costs (17.4%).
- Defined-risk structures were not shown by any snippet to be the paper's remedy. Whether spread margin (max-loss based) was modeled is a gap.

### Gaps
- Full text unreadable (hosts blocked). Not verified: exact moneyness grid, whether spreads/condors/butterflies are in the paper, spread/condor margin rule, published-version sample period, holding-period definition, whether costs were applied as a fraction of the quoted spread, and the exact table values behind 1.83 / 0.16 / 0.454 / 0.474.
- No evidence retrieved on stop-loss variants.

## 2. Descendants and related work with realistic costs

### Takeaway
The follow-up literature I could reach (abstract level only) agrees directionally: put-writing profits are large gross but shrink greatly with bid-ask costs and margin constraints. I found no peer-reviewed paper that optimizes iron-condor wing width or delta net of costs.

### Cited Findings
- Hong, Sung & Yang (2018), "On profitability of volatility trading on S&P 500 equity index options: The role of trading frictions", International Review of Economics & Finance 55, 295-307. Abstract only: significant profits on put-writing, but "our findings cast doubt on whether these profits can be genuinely attained in practice"; after bid-ask spreads "the profitability is significantly reduced"; margin requirements force a large fraction of wealth into margin accounts with "a high likelihood of margin calls"; profitability hinges on capturing the VRP and timing — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/reveco/v55y2018icp295-307.html), [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1059056017305191). US, index, SPX. No numbers retrieved. Strongly echoes Santa-Clara & Saretto, but may be a descendant rather than independent evidence.
- Constantinides, Czerwonko, Jackwerth & Perrakis (2011), "Are Options on Index Futures Profitable for Risk Averse Investors? Empirical Evidence", Journal of Finance 66(4), 1407-1437. American options on S&P 500 index futures, 1983-2006. Stochastic-dominance bounds. Writing options that violate the upper bound raises expected utility of a risk-averse investor holding market plus cash, after transaction costs and bid-ask spreads; call bid prices violate the bound more often than put bids; ask-price violations of lower bounds are rare; waits one quote before entering. Abstract/summary only — [NBER w16302](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16302), [Konstanz PDF listing](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/84f97218-3126-4794-8b09-42df3b6f5e0f). This is a mild conflict with the "profits vanish" framing: here, writing violating options survives costs, but selection is via a stochastic-dominance rule and not a plain systematic strangle.
- Faias & Santa-Clara, "Optimal Option Portfolio Strategies: Deepening the Puzzle of Index Option Mispricing", Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 52(1) (2017). Out-of-sample European-option portfolio held to maturity, with transaction costs: Sharpe 0.82 (published abstract); the 2011 working paper reports out-of-sample January 1996 to September 2008 with costs annualized Sharpe 0.59. Return said to come mainly from exploiting mispricing between options rather than jump or volatility risk premia, with positive skewness. Abstract/snippet only — [JFQA IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v52y2017i01p277-303_00.html), [working paper PDF](https://ecomod.net/system/files/OOPS2011Jan.pdf), [UNL repository](https://run.unl.pt/bitstreams/b7ad96e4-2191-4216-8a9d-cf551364d64f/download). A later MSc thesis re-testing at monthly/weekly/decomposed-weekly frequencies reports Sharpe 0.57 to 2.29 and says some return comes from jump and volatility risk premia, which contradicts the original attribution — [UNL thesis listing, via search summary]. Thesis is low-authority.
- Bondarenko, "Why Are Put Options So Expensive?", Quarterly Journal of Finance (2014): abstract-level only; reported that systematic sale of one-month unhedged ATM index puts earns large returns with highly significant alpha (2004 working-paper description, secondary). Bondarenko's January 2016 CBOE PUT / WPUT study: gross annualized Sharpe 0.67 and 0.47; weekly rollover may carry higher transaction costs — [CXO summary](https://cxoadvisory.com/?p=28335), [CBOE note](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/new-research-shows-options-based-strategies-can-generate-higher-gross-premiums-with-less-volatility-over-traditional-asset-classes). The user said not to re-research CBOE benchmark material; included only as a cost-caveat pointer.
- Hill, Balasubramanian, Gregory & Tierens (2006), "Finding Alpha via Covered Index Writing", Financial Analysts Journal 62(5), 29-46: covered S&P 500 call strategies outperformed with lower standard deviation over 15+ years; alternative overwriting structures adjust the written strike by volatility environment — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ufajxx/v62y2006i5p29-46.html). Abstract only; no cost figures retrieved. Industry-sponsored (OIC link).
- Wysocki (2026), arXiv 2608.24786, "Harvesting the Volatility Risk Premium: A Learning-to-Rank Approach": SPXW zero-DTE, LightGBM LambdaRank over nine daily candidates (eight delta-targeted short-put positions plus SKIP), path-aware Sortino label at one-minute resolution, margin-aware sizing, abstention rule, 2021-2024 walk-forward and held-out 2025. Abstract only (arxiv blocked) — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2608.24786.html). Single-leg short puts, not condors; useful as a design precedent for delta-bucket selection plus margin-aware sizing plus abstention. Results not retrieved. Note the arXiv id/date is as returned by the search tool; I could not independently check it.
- Stochastic optimal control of iron condor portfolios, arXiv 2501.12397: includes validation on SPX OptionMetrics data; in the bullish scenario only the largest-x portfolio hit maximum profit and 3 of 5 portfolios lost money. Snippet only; delta, wings and costs not covered — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12397).
- CBOE-published research paper on option strategies in a 60/40 portfolio: iron condors gave returns and risk similar to a traditional 60/40 portfolio, and the long iron condor had the highest total risk among strategies tested. Snippet only; not peer-reviewed — [CBOE PDF](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/education/research_publications/research-paper.pdf).
- India: a Journal of Investment Strategies (Risk.net) paper "Strangle to resuscitate: evidence from India" tests long and short strangles on Nifty, monthly data 2007-2019; short strangles beat long strangles on success rate, points earned and risk-adjusted return. Snippet did not mention transaction costs or a Sharpe figure — [Risk.net](https://www.risk.net/journal-of-investment-strategies/7721271/strangle-to-resuscitate-evidence-from-india). Only Nifty-specific academic hit; cost treatment unknown.

### Inferences
- Costs and margin findings are consistent across Santa-Clara & Saretto, Hong-Sung-Yang and the CBOE weekly-roll caveat, but several of these share US SPX data and overlapping authorship/lineage, so they are not independent confirmations.
- Faias & Santa-Clara and Constantinides et al. show that net-of-cost profits can survive when the trade is selected by a mispricing or dominance criterion rather than by fixed moneyness. That favors rule-based filtering over blind periodic selling.

### Gaps
- No full-text verification for any descendant paper. No SSRN/arXiv paper found that tests iron condors or butterflies with real bid-ask spreads and delta or wing-width grids. Driessen-Maenhout and Jones queries returned nothing usable. No quantitative Nifty cost-adjusted result from a peer-reviewed source.

## 3. Evidence on strike distance, days to expiry and wing width

### Takeaway
Gross risk-adjusted returns rise with OTM distance and nearer maturity, but so do round-trip costs and margin per dollar of premium, so the net-of-cost optimum is much less extreme. There is no peer-reviewed evidence retrieved on wing width; only practitioner statements of uncertain quality.

### Cited Findings
- Strike distance: near-maturity 10% OTM strangle Sharpe 0.654 vs ATM straddle 0.247 (gross; units unclear) — [search summary of Santa-Clara & Saretto](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/option_strategies.pdf)
- Cost by moneyness and maturity: 5% OTM call round-trip cost 17.4% near maturity vs 2.7% far from expiration — [same family of summaries](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/finance/santa_clara_option.pdf)
- Margin by moneyness and maturity: $3.9 per premium dollar for far-maturity ATM calls vs $43.1 for near-maturity 10% OTM calls — [same](http://www.econ.yale.edu/~shiller/behfin/2005-04/santa-clara-saretto.pdf)
- Practitioner (not peer-reviewed, not verified): a tastylive study via a third-party blog suggests an optimal condor wing of about $10 on $100 underlyings (about $5 per $100) — [OptionsTradingIQ](https://optionstradingiq.com/iron-condor-wing-width/); another tastylive note says wider wings did not add much premium, and defined-risk condors give up some decay and need about five more days to reach a 50% profit target than strangles — [tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/news-insights/strategies-to-profit-from-increased-market-volatility), [tastylive market measures](https://tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/rate-of-reaching-fifty-percent-01-15-2019). Vendor backtests claim 16-delta short strikes are the best risk-adjusted trade-off ([apexvol](https://apexvol.com/strategies/iron-condor/backtest), [OptionsPilot](https://optionspilot.app/blog/iron-condor-backtesting-spy-historical-performance)); these are marketing material with unclear method. The user excluded tastylive/CBOE benchmarks as already covered, so this is listed only for completeness and low weight.
- Retail Bank Nifty iron-condor backtest (2017-2020) reportedly turned from small profit to loss after costs — [TradingQnA forum](https://tradingqna.com/t/is-iron-condor-a-profitable-option-trading-strategy/81708). Anecdotal, unverified.

### Inferences
- If Santa-Clara & Saretto's cost pattern holds for index options generally, buying far-OTM wings adds a high proportional-cost leg, since far-OTM and near-expiry options carry the widest relative spreads. This is a plausible but untested argument for narrow-to-moderate rather than very narrow wings only if the wing is cheap in absolute rupees; the retrieved evidence does not settle the sign.
- Spread margin (max loss) would be far below naked margin for a given credit, which in the paper's framework should reduce forced liquidation, but no snippet demonstrates this.

### Gaps
- No evidence found by delta bucket, DTE bucket or wing width in peer-reviewed, cost-adjusted form. The paper's per-moneyness and per-maturity tables were not read.

## 4. Replication, decay, sample dependence and conflicts

### Takeaway
Evidence is thin and heavily sample-dependent: all core US results rely on 1985-2006 data (a period that includes the 1987 crash in the futures-options sample), and later weekly-expiry and 0DTE work has not been replicated with full costs in what I could reach.

### Cited Findings
- Conflict on headline Sharpe: abstract-level summary says "as high as 1.69" annualized for a near-maturity strangle; CXO summary says 1.83 gross falling to 0.16 net — [UCLA/SSRN summary](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=681643) vs [CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/good-deals-in-broad-market-index-options/). Likely version differences; unresolved.
- Conflict on sample: 1985-2002 (futures options 1985-May 2001) vs 1985-April 2006 across versions — [Yale copy](http://www.econ.yale.edu/~shiller/behfin/2005-04/santa-clara-saretto.pdf) vs [NBER copy](https://conference.nber.org/confer/2005/bfs05/saretto.pdf).
- Conflict on frictions: Santa-Clara & Saretto and Hong-Sung-Yang say profits are largely not capturable; Constantinides et al. (2011) say writing violating options does improve utility net of spreads; Faias & Santa-Clara report Sharpe 0.59-0.82 net of costs for an optimized portfolio. Not directly comparable (different selection rules, futures vs index options, myopic optimization vs fixed rules).
- Attribution conflict: Faias & Santa-Clara attribute returns to option mispricing rather than jump/volatility risk premia; a later thesis finds some return from jump and volatility risk premia — [UNL thesis summary](https://run.unl.pt/bitstreams/b7ad96e4-2191-4216-8a9d-cf551364d64f/download).
- Hong-Sung-Yang title and abstract refer to "time-varying market conditions" (regime dependence) — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/reveco/v55y2018icp295-307.html).

### Inferences
- The post-2006 decay of the short-premium edge, the effect of weekly expiries, and Indian-market spreads are not covered by the core evidence and must be measured in-sample on Nifty chain data (the project's chain engine already prices at EOD settle with no bid/ask, which is the same limitation these papers warn about).

### Gaps
- No replication study of Santa-Clara & Saretto located. No out-of-sample (post-2006) test with costs located. No non-US index (other than the Nifty strangle paper without cost detail) located.

## Implications for a Nifty weekly/monthly iron condor/credit spread backtester

These are testable hypotheses derived from the reported findings, not established results. The source evidence is US SPX/futures options, 1985-2006 (dominant), read at abstract/summary level only.

1. **Charge a spread-based round-trip cost that rises with OTM distance and falls with DTE.** Paper evidence: 5% OTM call round trip 17.4% near maturity vs 2.7% far. Test slippage as a percentage of option price (or of the quoted spread), not a flat rupee amount, and run at least 1.0x/1.5x/2.0x scenarios (the project already does this in the chain engine). Count all four legs on entry and exit.
2. **Model margin explicitly and path-dependently.** Paper evidence: margin calls turned the average monthly return of a 5% OTM near-maturity strangle from +2.7% to -1.0%. In the backtester, test (a) SPAN-like margin for naked legs vs max-loss margin for condors, (b) forced liquidation when mark-to-market loss plus margin exceeds equity, and (c) report returns on margin capital, not on credit.
3. **Structure comparison grid (same short strikes):** short strangle vs iron condor vs credit spread (single side). The paper summaries do not show condor results, so this is open. Primary metrics: net-of-cost Sharpe, worst-month loss, and fraction of days margin utilization exceeds a cap. Hypothesis: defined risk reduces margin-call liquidations but costs extra spread on two wing legs.
4. **Strike/delta buckets.** Gross Sharpe rose with OTM distance (10% OTM strangle 0.654 vs ATM straddle 0.247 per summary). Test short-strike buckets (for example 5, 10, 16, 20, 30 delta) separately for weekly and monthly, and judge on net-of-cost and margin-adjusted returns, since cost and margin per premium dollar worsen with distance ($43.1 vs $3.9). Expect the net optimum to be less extreme than the gross optimum; confirm empirically.
5. **Wing width.** No academic evidence retrieved; practitioner claims (about 5% of underlying in points; wider wings add little premium) are unverified. Test wing widths as multiples of the short-strike offset or as a fixed-delta wing (for example long leg at 5 vs 10 delta, or 100/200/300/500 index points on Nifty) and record: credit, margin, cost of the wing legs in spread terms, max loss, and tail frequency. Check that far-OTM wings have non-zero volume/OI (the project's archive skips legs with 0 contracts and 0 OI, which biases toward optimistic wing availability).
6. **DTE.** Near-maturity OTM options had the highest gross Sharpe and the highest relative costs. Compare weekly (e.g. 3-8 DTE, per the project's gate) vs monthly entries with cost and margin applied; do not assume weekly benefits carry over from monthly US evidence.
7. **Regime/timing filter.** Hong-Sung-Yang and Constantinides et al. indicate profits depend on conditions or selection. Test whether abstaining (VIX/IV-percentile, skew, margin-utilization gate) improves net Sharpe, treating each filter as a pre-registered hypothesis to avoid over-fitting.
8. **Report decay and sample splits.** Split results pre- and post-2019/2021 and by weekly-expiry availability; compare to the 1985-2006 US pattern only qualitatively.

**Caveats:** (i) no full texts read; numbers are secondary and some conflict; (ii) US index/futures options, mostly pre-2007; the Nifty market has different tick sizes, retail participation, weekly-expiry dominance and the Thursday-to-Tuesday expiry change; (iii) EOD settle data have no bid/ask, so the paper's cost methodology cannot be replicated exactly; (iv) the Faias-Santa-Clara and Constantinides results suggest selection rules matter, which raises data-snooping risk for any wing/delta grid search.
