# Volatility Risk Premium (VRP) Literature — Why Index Option Selling Has Had Positive EV, and When It Fails

## What is the volatility/variance risk premium — empirical and theoretical evidence

### Takeaway
Multiple independent academic strands — delta-hedged option return studies, variance-swap/synthetic-variance-swap studies, and model-free implied-vs-realized-variance studies — converge on the same finding for S&P 500 index options: implied (risk-neutral) volatility/variance has historically priced in more variance than subsequently materializes, i.e. there is a persistent, statistically robust, *negative* variance risk premium (volatility sellers are on average compensated for taking on variance exposure). This is one of the most replicated results in derivatives empirical finance, though the size of the premium is time-varying and not a free lunch.

### Cited Findings
- Bakshi & Kapadia (2003), "Delta-Hedged Gains and the Negative Market Volatility Risk Premium," *Review of Financial Studies* 16(2):527-566 — delta-hedged S&P 500 index option portfolios earn significantly negative average returns; the shortfall is largest for at-the-money options and during high-volatility periods, and smaller for out-of-the-money options. The authors interpret this as direct evidence that the market *volatility* risk premium is negative (i.e., option sellers who delta-hedge are compensated). — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v16y2003i2p527-566.html)
- Carr & Wu (2009), "Variance Risk Premiums," *Review of Financial Studies* 22(3):1311-1341 (working paper circulated as "Variance Risk Premia") — construct the variance risk premium as the gap between realized variance and a synthetic variance-swap rate built from a portfolio of options (model-free, matching the VIX-construction methodology); study five stock indexes and 35 individual stocks and find a historically negative variance risk premium for the indexes (option-implied variance > subsequently realized variance). — [NYU PDF](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf), [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v22y2009i3p1311-1341..html)
- Bollerslev, Tauchen & Zhou (2009), "Expected Stock Returns and Variance Risk Premia," *Review of Financial Studies* — define the VRP as the difference between model-free option-implied variance and high-frequency-based realized variance, and show this VRP *predicts* aggregate U.S. equity returns, especially at a roughly quarterly horizon, dominating standard predictors such as P/E, default spread, and the consumption-wealth ratio. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=948309)
- Bollerslev, Marrone, Xu & Zhou (2014), *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* — test whether the BTZ (2009) predictability is a small-sample statistical artifact; conclude it is not, and additionally document similar VRP-based return predictability across several other countries, with a "global" VRP showing even stronger predictive power in panel regressions. — [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/732839)
- Eraker (cited via secondary summary), using daily S&P 500 returns and VIX data 1990–2007 — one-month at-the-money index options implied roughly 19% annualized volatility on average versus ~15.7% realized, i.e. implied vol priced roughly 18% "too high" relative to what later occurred; the premium itself varies with the level of volatility. — [cxoadvisory.com summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/the-why-of-the-volatility-risk-premium)
- A CFA-Institute-digested study (using AQR/OptionMetrics/CBOE/S&P data, 1996–2014) defines the premium as VIX minus subsequent realized S&P 500 volatility, buckets by VIX decile, and finds the premium is positive in every decile; a delta-hedged long-put strategy 5% out-of-the-money lost money in every VIX bucket (i.e., buying downside protection was a net cost in every regime studied). — [CFA Institute digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2015/12/still-not-cheap-portfolio-protection-in-calm-markets-digest-summary) (secondary; original study not independently retrieved)
- Barras & Malkhozov (follow-on to Carr & Wu) — test whether the *option-implied* variance risk premium and an *equity-market-based* variance risk premium are statistically identical; reject that null, finding the gap between them is strongly related to the financial condition of derivatives-market intermediaries (i.e. the option-implied VRP partly reflects dealer/intermediary balance-sheet constraints, not purely end-investor risk aversion). — [search summary citing Malkhozov](https://www.systemicrisk.ac.uk/sites/default/files/media/Malkhozov_0.pdf)
- CBOE benchmark-index research: over 1990–2018 the BXM (covered call) index returned ~8.1%/yr vs. 9.3%/yr for the S&P 500 but with lower volatility (10.1% vs 14.2%); the PUT (cash-secured put-write) index returned ~10.1%/yr vs 10.3% for the S&P 500 from mid-1986–2017 while taking on roughly 66% of the index's risk (per Asset Consulting Group's update). CBOE states SPX put-writing's realized premium (implied > realized vol) held in most years over two decades, the chief exception being 2008. — [CBOE insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/key-cboe-benchmark-indexes-using-spx-options-offer-strong-risk-adjusted-returns/), [Nordea-hosted CBOE white paper](https://www.nb.com/-/media/NB/Article-Assets/wp_index_option_writing_strategy_benchmarks.pdf), [Asset Consulting Group update](https://acgnet.com/getattachment/845363f3-97c7-4ac9-be31-762e60b9143d/BXM-PUT-Update;.aspx)
- Cross-market caveat: a KOSPI200 study applying the Bakshi–Kapadia delta-hedging methodology did **not** find a significant at-the-money volatility risk premium in the Korean index-option market, though out-of-the-money puts did show significantly negative delta-hedged gains (partly attributed to overpriced puts / microstructure effects). This shows the premium's size and even existence is market-structure-dependent, not a universal law. — [Emerald/JDQS abstract](https://www.emerald.com/jdqs/article/17/2/67/205260/The-Volatility-Risk-Premium-in-the-KOSPI200-Index)

### Inferences
- The VRP/VRP-predictability literature is genuinely convergent across different measurement approaches (delta-hedged P&L, synthetic variance swaps, model-free IV vs. realized vol), which is stronger evidence than any single study — this is not a fragile, one-paper result for U.S./developed index options.
- The premium is NOT constant: it scales with the volatility level itself (bigger absolute premium in high-vol regimes per Eraker) and is time-varying enough to forecast returns (BTZ), meaning a naive "always short vol, fixed size" strategy ignores information the premium itself contains.
- The Barras–Malkhozov intermediary-constraint finding is important context for an options-selling strategy: part of the measured "edge" may reflect compensation dealers/sellers earn for balance-sheet and funding risk, not pure insurance-demand risk aversion — this matters for how robust the edge is when intermediary capacity changes (e.g. post-2018 vol-ETP unwind, dealer de-risking in stress).

## Economic rationale: why does the premium exist and persist

### Takeaway
The dominant academic/practitioner explanation is that index put (and more broadly variance) prices embed a persistent "insurance premium" that risk-averse end-investors and institutions (pension funds, insurers, portfolio managers) are willing to pay to hedge crash/left-tail risk, compounded by crash/skew aversion, intermediary/dealer funding constraints, and (per some practitioner sources) behavioral overpayment for tail protection; option sellers are effectively paid for underwriting this demand.

### Cited Findings
- Acadian Asset Management research note ("Crash Risk: Hedgers vs. Harvesters") — investors demand incremental expected return to bear infrequent-but-large-loss ("crash") risk; more negatively skewed strategies/assets show higher realized Sharpe ratios across equities, credit, and hedge funds, consistent with a priced crash-risk/skew premium. Cites Bollerslev & Todorov (2011) attributing a large share of the equity premium itself to rare-disaster risk. — [Acadian viewpoint](https://www.acadian-asset.com/investment-insights/managing-risk/crash-risk-hedgers-vs-harvesters)
- Practitioner/academic framing: the VRP is described as a premium paid by option buyers to option sellers for insurance-like protection against large adverse moves, with "crash aversion" and skew demand cited as a structural driver that pushes up put prices specifically (not just at-the-money implied vol generally). — [cxoadvisory summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/the-why-of-the-volatility-risk-premium)
- Institutional/dealer hedging feedback: research on 1980s-style portfolio insurance notes dynamic/delta hedging requires selling into falling markets and buying into rising markets, a trend-following flow pattern that itself can amplify realized volatility; this creates a structural, mechanical source of demand for crash protection beyond pure risk-aversion. — [search synthesis citing Acadian/JLEM sources](https://www.jlem.com/documents/FG/jlem/articles/580195_RiskAvoidance.pdf)
- Insurance-company channel: a Wharton/Jacobs Levy working paper ("Insurance against Long Run Volatility Risk") argues life insurers provide households with long-run volatility insurance via minimum-return guarantees, and then hedge that exposure in derivatives markets — a structural institutional source of persistent volatility-hedging demand that pushes up long-run volatility prices. — [Jacobs Levy Center PDF](https://jacobslevycenter.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/2019.12-Insurance-against-Long-Run-Volatility-Risk.pdf)
- Barras & Malkhozov (noted above) tie the size of the option-implied VRP to the financial health of derivatives-market intermediaries — i.e. dealers' capacity/willingness to warehouse short-vol risk is itself a priced, time-varying factor, not simply end-investor risk aversion. — [systemicrisk.ac.uk PDF](https://www.systemicrisk.ac.uk/sites/default/files/media/Malkhozov_0.pdf)
- Theoretical "skew premium" framing (general derivatives-theory summary): an option seller bears the risk of arbitrarily large losses, so the P&L distribution of the seller/insurer is inherently negatively skewed; standard asset-pricing logic says sellers of negatively-skewed payoffs should demand a compensating premium ("skew premium") for agreeing to bear that asymmetric risk. — [arXiv "Risk Premia: Asymmetric Tail Risks and Excess Returns"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.7720)
- Euan Sinclair, *Volatility Trading* — argues a trader should be able to explain **why** implied volatility is priced where it is as a precondition for trading it (if you can't articulate the specific edge, don't trade it); he states that behavioral biases are "probably the drivers behind most sources of edge available to a volatility trader," i.e. a behavioral-overpayment explanation sits alongside the risk-premium explanation. — [publisher/retailer description synthesis](https://www.ebook.de/de/product/21158403/euan_sinclair_volatility_trading.html)

### Inferences
- The literature does not present one single cause; it layers (a) classic risk-aversion/insurance demand, (b) a mechanical/structural hedging-flow channel (dynamic hedging, insurer guarantee books) that is somewhat independent of individual risk aversion, and (c) dealer/intermediary capacity constraints that make the premium's size partly a "liquidity provision" compensation rather than pure insurance economics. Any single-cause explanation ("people are just irrationally scared") is likely incomplete per the academic sources.
- Because part of the premium (per Barras–Malkhozov) reflects intermediary constraints rather than stable end-investor preferences, the premium's magnitude should be expected to fluctuate with dealer balance-sheet stress (e.g., widens in funding-stress periods, not just high-realized-vol periods) — this is a subtler driver than "crash insurance demand" alone and is relevant to any "when does it break" analysis.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve a rigorous academic decomposition quantifying how much of the historical premium is "pure risk aversion" vs. "behavioral overpayment" vs. "dealer constraint compensation" — the literature discusses all three but a clean attribution/variance-decomposition study was not found in this pass.

## What options-education books say: Natenberg, Cordier & Gross

### Takeaway
Natenberg's *Option Volatility and Pricing* is the standard quantitative/probabilistic reference teaching that an option seller's edge comes from systematically selling when implied volatility is priced above one's best estimate of future realized volatility, with explicit emphasis on vega and theta risk management rather than naive "high probability of profit" reasoning; Cordier & Gross's *The Complete Guide to Option Selling* is a popular, non-technical practitioner book advocating selling (often naked) out-of-the-money options for steady premium income, and real-world events — the November 2018 blow-up of co-author James Cordier's own fund, OptionSellers.com — are widely cited as a cautionary, directly-on-point case study of the strategy's tail risk.

### Cited Findings
- Publisher/secondary descriptions of Natenberg's *Option Volatility and Pricing* confirm the book's core content: option pricing models, the distinction between historical, future, and implied volatility, the limitations of the Black-Scholes-Merton model (constant-volatility and constant-rate assumptions that "rarely hold in real markets"), and volatility skew/surface behavior across strikes and expirations. — [search synthesis of publisher/retailer listings](https://mcsprogram.org/uploaded-files/u4682G/245091/Natenberg%20Options%20Volatility%20And%20Pricing.pdf) (note: several hits were low-quality unofficial mirror/summary PDFs, flagged as lower-reliability and not relied on for substantive claims beyond these generic, multiply-corroborated points)
- Note on source reliability: a dedicated search for Natenberg's specific "probability-based edge," vega/theta risk framing, and selling-options discussion did not surface primary book text (only publisher blurbs and generic AI-style mirror-site summaries); this is flagged below as a **gap** rather than asserted as fact.
- Cordier & Gross, *The Complete Guide to Option Selling* (1st ed. 2004, 2nd ed. ~2009, McGraw-Hill) — publisher/library-catalog listings describe it as "the only book that explores selling options exclusively," including chapters on "SPAN margin: the key to high returns" and "Risk control in option selling." — [McGraw-Hill product page](https://www.mheducation.com/highered/mhp/product/complete-guide-option-selling.html), [library catalog chapter listing](https://librarycatalogue.taylors.edu.my/bib/50883)
- No independent critical/academic review of the book's specific strategy claims was found in this search pass — results returned were overwhelmingly marketing copy, library catalog entries, and an unsubstantive Goodreads rating page. This is noted as a **gap**, not filled with speculation.
- **James Cordier / OptionSellers.com blow-up (November 2018):** Natural gas spiked sharply in early-to-mid November 2018, including a one-day ~20% surge on November 14 to an intraday high near $4.93; OptionSellers.com — a firm run by Cordier specializing in writing (selling) commodity options for high-net-worth clients — lost roughly $150 million across ~290 client accounts, which were forcibly liquidated by its clearing broker, INTL FCStone. — [optionstradingiq.com account](https://optionstradingiq.com/how-to-lose-100-million/), [Wolf Street market commentary](https://wolfstreet.com/2018/12/17/us-natural-gas-plunges-unwinds-spike-that-blew-up-hedge-fund/)
- Cordier publicly addressed the losses in a YouTube video, describing a "rogue wave" in the natural gas market and stating the swings "likely cost me my hedge fund." — [ThinkAdvisor coverage](https://thinkadvisor.com/2018/11/21/wiped-out-hedge-fund-manager-confesses-losses-on-youtube)
- Commentators drew a direct line to the strategy's structural risk: one noted that writing naked options in the volatile natural gas market was "like placing your first-born in the middle of a busy freeway"; another pointed out Cordier himself had written a Futures-magazine article months earlier (May 2018) warning about the perils of trading the gas market — underscoring that the risk was known, not merely a surprise "black swan." Clients also reportedly faced potential liability beyond their total account losses to the clearing broker. — [theshortbear.substack.com account](https://theshortbear.substack.com/p/blowing-up-selling-options)
- A 2024 retrospective (Benzinga) explicitly invokes the Cordier blow-up as a cautionary precedent when discussing the then-current trend of selling far-out-of-the-money SPX puts for a few cents of premium, quoting a volatility trader warning that "tail selling is returning at larger size than in the past decade." — [Benzinga](https://benzinga.com/short-sellers/24/04/38086212/picking-up-pennies-in-front-of-a-steamroller-investors-who-dont-learn-from-the-past-are-doomed-to-r)

### Inferences
- The Cordier episode is a textbook real-world illustration of the "sell options for steady income until a tail event" failure mode the academic VRP/skew-premium literature predicts structurally (negatively skewed payoff → rare catastrophic loss can exceed many periods' cumulative gains) — it is not an isolated anecdote but is treated by practitioner commentary as the canonical case study for undiversified/naked commodity option selling.
- The fact that Cordier had himself written publicly about natural-gas tail risk before his own fund's collapse is repeatedly highlighted by critics as evidence that *awareness* of tail risk is not the same as *risk-sizing/position-limiting* against it — a distinction directly relevant to any "concrete strategy and risk rules" claims the book makes vs. what was actually practiced.

## When the volatility risk premium shrinks, reverses, or becomes unreliable

### Takeaway
The premium is documented to compress or reverse during realized-vol spikes/crash periods (academic sources note 2008 as the chief historical exception year for SPX put-writing), and — separately from the average-premium question — short-volatility strategies face a distinct, well-documented "crowded trade" / structural fragility risk, most dramatically realized in the February 2018 "Volmageddon" event, in which short-vol exchange-traded products lost over 90% of value in a single day via a mechanical feedback loop; more broadly, academic and practitioner sources agree that a negatively-skewed payoff structure means a single severe loss can exceed the cumulative gains of dozens of winning periods, so average/expected-value reasoning is insufficient for leveraged or undiversified sellers who face path-dependent ruin risk.

### Cited Findings
- CBOE benchmark research states that SPX put-writing's realized premium (implied vol priced above subsequently realized vol) held in most years over two decades, with **2008** being the chief historical exception — i.e., the global financial crisis year is the explicitly documented instance where the premium reversed/was overwhelmed by realized volatility. — [CBOE insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/key-cboe-benchmark-indexes-using-spx-options-offer-strong-risk-adjusted-returns/)
- CFA Institute Financial Analysts Journal (2021), summary of an academic paper on "Volmageddon" — a sudden VIX spike of a record ~20 points on February 5, 2018 caused short-volatility exchange-traded products to lose more than 90% of their value in a single day; the paper attributes this to a feedback loop in which rising futures prices forced further short-vol-ETP buying/covering, shrinking (and then collapsing) their assets under management, which further fed the spiral. — [search-engine synthesis of CFA Institute FAJ abstract](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2021/volmageddon-failure-short-volatility-products) (note: direct WebFetch of this page failed with a DNS error; the finding is reported here only via the search-result synthesis, not verified against the primary abstract text — flagged as moderate-confidence)
- Mechanics of the XIV ETN's termination (Feb 2018): XIV had ~$1.8 billion AUM at end-January 2018; the VIX spike drove XIV's intraday indicative value down to ≤20% of its prior close, triggering Credit Suisse's contractual "acceleration event" (forced termination); post-event AUM in the vehicle fell to under $100 million. — [Bloomberg/BloombergQuint "The Day The Vix Doubled"](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2019-02-06/the-day-the-vix-doubled-tales-of-volmageddon)
- Litigation note: a New York appeals court ruled Credit Suisse must face allegations that it engineered XIV's implosion via its own VIX-futures trading — these remain allegations, not adjudicated findings, and are noted here with that caveat. — [ETF.com coverage](https://www.etf.com/sections/news/vix-etns-closure-accelerated)
- Aftermath: combined assets in volatility-linked ETPs had roughly halved to ~$2.69 billion in the years following 2018, with some issuers subsequently modifying investment objectives to reduce risk — evidence of a durable structural de-risking of the short-vol-ETP space after the event, not just a one-day shock. — [fxempire retrospective](https://fxempire.com/news/article/four-years-after-volmageddon-new-volatility-etfs-to-hit-market-950345)
- Taleb's long-standing critique (via *Fooled by Randomness* and subsequent commentary): "Option sellers, it is said, eat like chickens and go to the bathroom like elephants" — a vivid statement of the core tail-risk critique, that steady small gains from selling options can be wiped out by one catastrophic move; Taleb's own trading approach was reportedly the mirror image — buying the deep out-of-the-money options others were advised to sell, accepting that most expire worthless in exchange for convex payoff in rare events. — [Wikipedia summary of *Fooled by Randomness*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fooled_by_Randomness)
- A formal academic framing of the same point: because an option seller/insurer's P&L is "almost by definition" negatively skewed, sellers should rationally demand a compensating "skew premium" — but this is precisely the mechanism that makes average/expected returns a poor single statistic for risk assessment, since the distribution's mean is pulled up by frequent small gains while the tail risk of ruin lives in the (rare) left tail. — [arXiv "Risk Premia: Asymmetric Tail Risks and Excess Returns"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.7720)
- AQR/Ilmanen (2013) published a direct rebuttal to Taleb's tail-hedging claims, arguing Taleb's confidence in his priors was "excessive" and that "we do not have enough data to warrant" strong conclusions either way, characterizing some of Taleb's claims as "plausible speculations supported by anecdotes rather than empirical analysis" — illustrating that the magnitude/reliability of the tail-risk critique itself is academically contested, not a settled matter. This dispute became public again in 2020 in a Twitter exchange between Taleb and AQR's Cliff Asness. — [AQR author response](https://www.aqr.com/insights/research/journal-article/do-financial-markets-reward-buying-or-selling-insurance-and-lottery-tickets-author-response), [Bloomberg/FA-Mag coverage of 2020 dispute](https://www.fa-mag.com/news/black-swan-author-spars-with-quant-legend-over-tail-risk-hedges-55887.html?section=3)
- Pepperdine Graziadio working paper on SPY short strangles found that strangle-selling is usually profitable period-by-period, but that losses on a single bad position can equal the cumulative profit of roughly **forty** prior winning positions — a concrete, quantified statement of the "small-steady-gains, rare-catastrophic-loss" payoff asymmetry underlying the general critique. — [Pepperdine Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.pepperdine.edu/graziadiowps/25)
- On leverage/ruin mechanics more generally (not VRP-specific, but directly applicable to why "average expectancy" is insufficient for leveraged/undiversified sellers): sources note that in a genuine panic correlations move toward 1 (diversification fails exactly when it is most needed), and that leverage "converts a survivable drawdown into a forced exit at the bottom" — i.e., a strategy with positive long-run/ensemble expected value can still force path-dependent ruin for a leveraged participant who cannot survive to realize the long-run average (consistent with, though not citing directly, the general ergodicity/Kelly-criterion point that time-averaged and ensemble-averaged outcomes diverge under leverage and fat tails). A Journal of Investment Strategies paper separately shows that for assets with negative skew and positive excess kurtosis (fat tails), the (fractional) Kelly-optimal bet size is mechanically reduced relative to a thin-tailed asset — formal confirmation that fat-tailed/negatively-skewed payoffs (exactly the short-option payoff shape) require smaller position sizing to avoid long-run ruin, even when the average per-trade edge is genuinely positive. — [general risk-education synthesis](https://www.dividendvision.com/learn/tail-risk), [Journal of Investment Strategies / risk.net "Leverage and Uncertainty"](https://www.risk.net/journal-of-investment-strategies/5330296/leverage-and-uncertainty)

### Inferences
- There are two analytically distinct "failure modes" documented in the sources, and both matter for an options-selling strategy design: (1) the *average* premium itself shrinking or reversing in realized high-vol/crash periods (the 2008 exception cited by CBOE), which is a statement about the mean of the return distribution; and (2) a structurally different "crowded trade" / mechanical-feedback fragility (Volmageddon/XIV) that can produce catastrophic single-day losses even independent of whether the long-run premium "exists" — this is a statement about tail/liquidity dynamics and product structure (daily-rebalanced leveraged/inverse vol products), not about the VRP's sign.
- The quantitative "40 winners wiped out by 1 loser" Pepperdine finding and the formal fractional-Kelly-under-fat-tails result together give a reasonably rigorous answer to "why does average expectancy become irrelevant for leveraged/undiversified sellers": a genuinely positive arithmetic-mean edge per trade is compatible with near-certain eventual ruin under naive fixed/growing position sizing because the loss distribution's left tail is fat and the gain distribution's right tail is thin — classic negative-skew asymmetry. This is the rigorous-academic underpinning for the popular "picking up nickels in front of a steamroller" metaphor, which is otherwise an informal/practitioner framing (Taleb et al.) rather than a citation-backed statistic on its own.
- The AQR/Ilmanen vs. Taleb dispute is a useful caveat for the report writer: even the *degree* to which tail risk should dominate decision-making for option sellers is contested among serious, credentialed practitioners/academics — it should not be presented as a unanimous view that short-vol strategies are irrational, only that the tail risk is real, documented, and historically realized (Cordier, Volmageddon), while its proper risk-adjusted pricing is debated.

### Gaps
- The direct WebFetch of the CFA Institute Financial Analysts Journal "Volmageddon" paper page failed (DNS resolution error on the proxy); the finding above is sourced only via a search-engine-generated synthesis of the abstract, not the verified primary text — flagged as moderate- rather than high-confidence and should be corroborated by the report writer if the specific loss percentage is used as a hard figure.
- No academic paper specifically modeling "crowded short-vol trade" effects on the *level* of the VRP itself (as opposed to single-event crash mechanics) was retrieved — e.g., whether large systematic inflows into short-vol strategies have measurably compressed the average VRP over time is asserted informally by practitioner sources (Risk.net's "hedge funds pile into short volatility QIS options") but not quantified by a peer-reviewed study in this search pass.
- Did not find a dedicated academic ergodicity-economics (Ole Peters-style) paper applied specifically to option-selling; the ruin/leverage argument above is supported by adjacent Kelly-criterion/skew-kurtosis literature and general risk-education sources rather than a single definitive paper on this exact framing.

## Other influential books/practitioner resources on systematic option selling

### Takeaway
Beyond Natenberg and Cordier & Gross, the most frequently cited practitioner/quant resource is Euan Sinclair's *Volatility Trading* (Wiley, 1st ed. 2008; 2nd ed. adds a chapter explicitly on "trading the variance premium"), which insists on a clearly articulable statistical edge and flags behavioral biases as the probable source of most tradeable edges; tastytrade/tastylive (Tom Sosnoff's firm) has published a large body of in-house backtests on systematic premium selling (iron condors, strangles) generally finding positive long-run expectancy that improves with IV-rank-based entry filters and active win-management, though these are self-published, non-peer-reviewed, promotional studies rather than independent academic research and should be labeled as such.

### Cited Findings
- Euan Sinclair, *Volatility Trading* (Wiley; 2nd edition adds chapters on "the dynamics of realized and implied volatilities, trading the variance premium, and using options to trade special situations in equity markets") — presents a quantitative framework for measuring volatility to find an edge, emphasizing that a trader must be able to explain *why* implied volatility is priced where it is as a precondition for trading it, and that psychological/behavioral biases are "probably the drivers behind most sources of edge available to a volatility trader." Also stresses position-sizing by projected return/risk relative to the trader's goals. — [publisher/retailer synthesis](https://www.ebook.de/de/product/21158403/euan_sinclair_volatility_trading.html), [Wiley product page](https://gov.wiley.com/WileyCDA/WileyTitle/productCd-0470181990.html)
- tastylive/tastytrade in-house research (self-published, not peer-reviewed): a 2016 SPY iron-condor study found a baseline win rate of ~53%; filtering to sell only when Implied Volatility Rank (IVR) > 35% raised the win rate to ~70% and sharply increased average realized P/L (from $9.95 to $36.51 per the study's figures). A 2015 "long-term premium selling" study across 16 underlyings (including commodities) over a 10-year window concluded that consistently selling premium over the long term "can indeed be profitable." A 2016 "managing winners" study on SPY one-standard-deviation strangles (2005+) found that closing positions early at 50% of max profit improved P/L, P/L-per-day, and win rate relative to holding to expiration. — [tastylive iron condor study](https://www.tastylive.com/shows/best-practices/episodes/zero-sum-10-16-2018), [tastylive research recap](https://tastylive.com/shows/research-recap/episodes/premium-selling-long-term-in-3-mins-or-less-08-04-2015), [tastylive managing winners study](https://www.tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/managing-winners-varying-profit-targets-12-04-2015)
- CBOE's own benchmark-index program (BXM covered-call, PUT cash-secured put-write, BXMD, CMBO) functions as a long-running, publicly-tracked, non-academic-but-rules-based practitioner resource/evidence base for systematic index option selling, with CBOE reporting higher Sharpe/Sortino ratios than the underlying index over 32.5 years, while flagging that the Sharpe ratio assumes normal returns and these strategies have negative skew — a direct, self-aware caveat from the index provider itself about the exact asymmetric-tail-risk issue discussed above. — [CBOE insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/benchmark-indices-series-income-generation-and-smoother-returns-with-cboes-bxm-bxmd-put-and-cmbo-indices)

### Inferences
- The tastytrade research and the CBOE benchmark indexes both constitute real, trackable, long-horizon practitioner evidence of positive expectancy from systematic index/ETF option selling, but neither is academically peer-reviewed; the report should clearly flag tastytrade's studies as promotional/in-house (produced by a brokerage that profits from options trading volume) even though their qualitative conclusions (IV-rank filtering helps; early profit-taking helps) are broadly consistent with what the academic VRP literature would predict (the premium is larger when implied vol is elevated, consistent with BTZ's time-varying VRP).
- Sinclair's explicit "you must know why the edge exists, or don't trade it" principle, combined with his point about behavioral biases being the probable source of most available edges, is a useful bridge between the rigorous academic risk-premium explanation and the softer "insurance demand/overpayment for tail protection" narrative — Sinclair effectively endorses the behavioral-overpayment channel as a practitioner-level heuristic.
